Does this contract verify the domain your wallet signs?

source

An EIP-712 type string is opaque data to the compiler. A typo in a domain field compiles, deploys, and silently breaks every signature: the wallet hashes what the spec says, the contract hashes what the typo says, and the digests never match. Nothing reverts.

This page recomputes the domain separator the way a wallet would and compares it to the contract's own DOMAIN_SEPARATOR(). It never says "safe" — when it cannot verify something, it says so.

A browser can only call endpoints that allow it (CORS). If the defaults are blocked, paste your own — a local node, Alchemy, Infura.

Pre-ERC-5267 contract? Supply its deployed name/version

Tokens like USDC and DAI expose DOMAIN_SEPARATOR() but not eip712Domain(), so there is nothing on-chain to read a name from. Supplying the name the contract was deployed with lets it be verified. A wrong name will show up as a mismatch — which is the point.

Paste what your wallet is showing you, before you sign it. The signature field is optional — with it, the signer is recovered.

Where do I find this? What if my chain isn't Ethereum?

Finding the request. When a site asks you to sign, your wallet shows a prompt with the fields and the contract address. Copy that JSON. If the site only shows you a summary, open your wallet's detailed view — the address is labelled verifyingContract.

Other chains. Put your chain's RPC endpoint in the box at the top first — the page checks that the endpoint's chain matches the chainId in the payload, and will refuse to pretend otherwise. For Robinhood Chain, that endpoint is rpc.mainnet.chain.robinhood.com and the chain id is 4663.

Chain names this page recognises are shown next to the connection status. An unrecognised chain still works, it just isn't named.

How to use this before you sign

  1. A dapp asks your wallet to sign. The request contains a verifyingContract — the contract address — and it is shown in the wallet prompt. Copy the whole request, or the JSON your wallet displays.
  2. Paste it into the Signed payload tab above.
  3. Read what this signature authorises. That is the part that can cost you money: an unlimited allowance hands over the whole balance.
  4. The domain check tells you whether the contract will accept the signature. It does not tell you the contract is honest.

A pass here is not a green light. A malicious contract can ask for an unlimited approval with a perfectly valid, on-chain-consistent domain — the signature verifies, and you are drained. The domain check catches broken contracts; only reading the fields catches hostile ones.

What this cannot tell you

  • It is not an oracle. "No mismatch detected" is not "safe".
  • A typo inside a message struct's type string (e.g. in Permit) is only caught if you supply a signature — then recovery fails and says so.
  • unverified and no_domain are not verdicts. They mean the tool could not see enough to judge either way.

Pure client-side — no server, no accounts, no analytics. Nothing you paste leaves the browser except the RPC calls needed to read the chain.
